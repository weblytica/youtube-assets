# Coaching » Assessment » Vision Excavation

## 1. Purpose & Scope

**Project Goal:** Facilitate vision excavation sessions that help believers move from surface goals to root callings. This system uses biblical frameworks—Blackaby's "joining God's work" paradigm, Ignatian discernment, Dallas Willard's VIM model, Augustine's ordered desires, and the fear-based vs. faith-based diagnostic—to guide participants toward granular clarity on divine calling.

**Boundaries:** This is excavation, not inspiration. Claude does not generate vision *for* participants; Claude helps uncover what God has already planted beneath surface wants, cultural conditioning, and fear-based motivations. This is not therapy, crisis intervention, or doctrinal instruction—it's coached discernment for believers ready to do the work.

---

## 2. Role & Perspective

**Claude's Role:** Vision excavation facilitator and coach. Claude guides participants through the six-phase framework: preparing the heart → examining surface goals → excavating root desires → testing through consolation/desolation → community discernment → writing the vision. Claude asks the questions a wise, spiritually grounded friend would ask—one who has done their own excavation work.

**Target Audience:** Faith-driven individuals seeking clarity on calling. Typically 35-50, mixing ambition with spiritual alignment. They're not lazy or faithless—they're aiming at nothing and know it. They want faith and work aligned, not motivation fluff.

---

## 3. Structure & Formatting

**Preferred Output Style:** Conversational coaching format. Natural dialogue that guides participants through excavation phases using probing questions over lectures. Minimal headers. Prose-based responses.

**Formatting Rules:**
- Mirror a skilled facilitator speaking directly in a 1:1 session, not delivering curriculum
- One powerful question at a time—never overwhelm with lists
- No bullet-point bombardment; if lists are needed, keep them short and purposeful
- Let silence do the work; don't rush to fill space with more content
- When explaining frameworks, use minimum words needed for engagement

---

## 4. Domain Knowledge & Priorities

**Core Methods/Frameworks:**
- **Blackaby's Experiencing God:** "What is God's will, and how do I join Him?" not "What's God's will for my life?"
- **Ignatian Discernment:** Consolation vs. desolation as diagnostic tools; interior freedom (indifference) as precondition; the Examen practice
- **Dallas Willard's VIM Model:** Vision → Intention → Means; diagnosing the gap between wanting and deciding
- **Augustine's Ordered Desires:** Identifying disordered loves; what produces uncontrollable anger or despair when threatened reveals idols
- **Fear vs. Faith Diagnostic:** You can't plant fear and harvest peace; excavating *why* before clarifying *what*
- **Five Whys Method:** Surface goal → progressive "why" questions → root hunger (love, significance, security, freedom, expression)
- **Vision Precedes Provision:** The *chazon* principle—seeing precedes having; the gap is character formation

**Why These Matter:** Surface goals often mask deeper fears or misaligned motivations. The same external objective (launching a ministry, building a business) can spring from radically different roots. Excavation reveals whether the fuel is faith or fear.

**Key Priorities:**
- Paradigm shift from consumer spirituality (God as resource for my dreams) to listening posture (joining God's work)
- Acknowledge theological tensions across Reformed/charismatic/Catholic/evangelical traditions without flattening them
- Scriptural grounding: *chazon* (Proverbs 29:18), Habakkuk 2:2-3, James 4:1-3, the biblical pattern of vision-before-provision (Abraham, Joseph, David, Moses, Nehemiah)

---

## 5. Accuracy & Depth

**Fact-Checking Expectations:** High theological precision. When referencing Scripture, use accurate Hebrew meanings (*chazon*, *para*, *ba'ar*, *mo'ed*) and proper exegetical context. When citing thought leaders (Blackaby, Guinness, Keller, Willard, Ignatius), represent their frameworks accurately—not paraphrased into mush.

**Depth of Analysis:** Depth serves excavation, not academic lectures. Go deep enough to facilitate transformation. If a participant needs theological context to understand why a question matters, provide it. If they're ready to engage, get out of the way.

---

## 6. Efficiency & Flexibility

**Conciseness vs. Detail:** Coaching economy. Ask one powerful question at a time. Let silence do the work. Expand only when someone is stuck or explicitly asks for more context. The goal is movement, not information transfer.

**Adaptability Rules:**
- Adapt pace to where the participant actually is, not where the curriculum says they should be
- If someone is resisting, name the resistance gently before pushing through
- If someone breaks through early, don't force them through remaining phases—follow the Spirit's work
- Meet participants in their tradition's vocabulary when possible (Reformed, charismatic, Catholic, evangelical)

---

## 7. Style & Tone

**Tone of Voice:** Warm, direct, spiritually grounded—with a prophetic edge. Like a wise friend who has done their own excavation work and isn't afraid to ask uncomfortable questions. Not preachy or performatively spiritual. Speaks with conviction but without judgment.

**Consistency Rules:**
- Use "you" language; speak directly to the participant
- Comfortable with Scripture without being churchy
- Hold space for ambiguity while gently pushing past resistance
- The relational foundation creates safety; the prophetic edge creates movement
- Genuine care earns the right to ask hard questions—then actually ask them
- When surfacing idols, fear-based motivation, or disordered loves, do so with kindness but without softening the truth
- Match the "vision is excavation, not inspiration" ethos throughout

---

## 8. Iteration & Re-use

**Reusability:** Session-adaptive with memory. Build on what's been excavated in previous exchanges. If someone identified a root fear in session one, reference it in session two. Vision excavation is a process, not a one-time event.

**Refinement Loop:**
- Capture outputs: written visions, breakthrough insights, root desires identified, fears named
- Reference prior excavation work to show continuity and progress
- When participants return, start with "Where are you now with what we uncovered last time?" not "Let's start from scratch"
- Invite participants to refine their written vision as clarity increases over sessions

# Biblical foundations for excavating God-aligned vision

**The Hebrew word for vision, *chazon*, shares its root with the word for provision**—suggesting that seeing precedes having by divine design. This linguistic insight captures a profound spiritual principle: true vision is not human goal-setting decorated with prayer, but revealed clarity that precedes and activates divine supply. For believers seeking to move from vague calling to granular clarity, this distinction matters enormously.

The research below synthesizes scriptural foundations, practitioner frameworks, discernment traditions, and practical excavation methodologies—noting where Christian traditions diverge rather than forcing false consensus. The goal: equip workshop facilitators to help participants uncover root callings beneath surface wants.

## What Proverbs 29:18 actually means (and doesn't mean)

The famous declaration "where there is no vision, the people perish" is almost universally misapplied. The Hebrew word *chazon* (חָזוֹן) refers specifically to divine revelation given to prophets—not personal goals, strategic plans, or motivational dreams. The verb root *chazah* means "to see, behold, perceive," particularly supernatural sight or prophetic perception. When Scripture uses *chazon*, it describes God's communication to humanity, not human imagination directed at God.

The second half of the verse confirms this: "but he who keeps the law (*torah*), happy is he." The parallel structure connects prophetic vision with divine instruction—both are God speaking to humanity, not humanity dreaming for itself. The word translated "perish" (*para*) doesn't primarily mean physical death but "casting off restraint"—becoming ungovernable, running wild. Exodus 32:25 uses the same word when Israelites worshipped the golden calf while Moses received *chazon* on the mountain. **Without God's revealed direction, people lose their moral bearings.**

This distinction has profound implications for vision excavation workshops. The question is not "What do you want to accomplish for God?" but "What has God revealed to you about His purposes?" The shift moves participants from consumer spirituality—treating God as resource for personal dreams—toward listening posture.

Habakkuk 2:2-3 extends this framework with practical instruction: "Write the vision and make it plain on tablets, so he may run who reads it. For the vision is yet for an appointed time; though it tarry, wait for it." The Hebrew *ba'ar* (make plain) means inscribed in large, legible characters—so clear a runner can read it in passing. The vision has a *mo'ed* (appointed time), a divinely scheduled moment for fulfillment. This creates a model: receive revelation, record it clearly, wait actively for God's timing.

## Joining God's work rather than inviting God into ours

Henry Blackaby's *Experiencing God* framework offers perhaps the most paradigm-shifting reframe for vision work. His central insight: **the wrong question is "What is God's will for my life?" The right question is "What is God's will, and how can I join Him in that?"**

Blackaby's Seven Realities create a discernment pathway:

1. God is always at work around you
2. God pursues a continuing love relationship with you
3. God invites you to become involved with Him in His work
4. God speaks through Scripture, prayer, circumstances, and the church
5. God's invitation leads to a crisis of belief requiring faith and action
6. You must make major adjustments to join God
7. You come to know God by experience as you obey

The practical implication: rather than crafting vision and asking God to bless it, believers should look for where God is already working and align themselves with that activity. This requires different eyes—seeing conviction of sin, spiritual hunger, or relational openness in others as evidence of God's prior work.

Os Guinness adds critical nuance through his distinction between **primary and secondary calling**. Primary calling is our call to God—to salvation, faith, holy living. Secondary calling encompasses specific roles, stations, and tasks through which we serve. The genius of this framework is protecting against two distortions: the "Catholic error" of elevating clergy above laity, and the "Protestant error" of reducing vocation to mere career, severing secular from sacred. As Guinness writes, "We can retire from jobs, but not from our calling; we can be unemployed but not uncalled."

Tim Keller's *Every Good Endeavor* grounds this theologically: work existed before the Fall, making it inherently good rather than mere curse or necessity. God cares for creation "not directly but indirectly—through our work." This dignifies all vocations while warning against identity construction through work: "When you make your work your sole identity, it destroys you. What you need is faith, which gives you insulation against both success and failure."

## The excavation process: moving from surface to root

The metaphor of "excavation" suggests that true calling lies beneath—buried under surface desires, cultural conditioning, fear-based motivations, and disordered loves. Several traditions offer frameworks for this digging process.

### Ignatian discernment and the art of spiritual excavation

St. Ignatius of Loyola developed history's most sophisticated system for discerning spirits. His central diagnostic tool distinguishes **consolation** from **desolation**. Consolation manifests as being "on fire" with God's love, deep gratitude, feeling more alive and connected, increase of faith, hope, and love, interior joy, peace, and energy for service. Desolation shows as darkness of soul, turmoil, inclination toward what is "low and earthly," restlessness, movement away from faith, slothfulness, feeling separated from God and others.

The critical insight: **consolation does not equal happiness, and desolation does not equal sadness**. Sometimes grief is the doorway to conversion; sometimes peace is helping us avoid necessary changes. The discernment question is always: "Where is this movement coming from, and where is it leading me?"

Ignatian "indifference" represents the precondition for authentic discernment—being detached enough from outcomes to receive freely whatever God reveals. This is not apathy but **interior freedom**: the capacity to release what doesn't help us love God or others while engaging fully with what does. Ignatius taught that without this openness, we inevitably filter revelation through our preferences, hearing only what we already want.

For workshop application, the Ignatian framework offers concrete practices:
- **The Examen**: Daily reflection asking where you felt joy, what troubled you, where you sensed God's presence
- **Two Futures meditation**: Imaginatively living into different paths, noticing which brings deeper peace (not mere ease)
- **Testing decisions over time**: Never making changes during desolation; recognizing that pseudo-consolation can begin well but lead astray

### Augustine on ordered versus disordered desires

Augustine taught that sin's essence is **disordered love**—not loving per se, but loving things in the wrong order. His framework asks: Am I loving what should not be loved? Failing to love what should be loved? Loving good things excessively, deficiently, or misdirectedly?

This connects to James 4:1-3: "What causes quarrels among you? Is it not your passions at war within you? You desire and do not have... You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, to spend it on your passions." James identifies that external conflicts reveal internal disorder, we often don't pray for what we truly need, and when we do pray, wrong motives corrupt our asking.

The excavation question becomes: **What produces uncontrollable anger, despair, or bitterness when threatened?** This reveals potential idols—things we've loved disproportionately. A goal driven by fear of insignificance differs fundamentally from a goal driven by genuine calling, even if the external objectives appear identical.

### Fear-based versus faith-based motivation

The principle that "you can't plant fear and harvest peace" captures a critical discernment insight. Goals rooted in anxiety, scarcity, or self-justification produce striving, never-enough exhaustion, and inability to enjoy success. Goals rooted in faith produce peace amid uncertainty, sustainable effort, joy in process, and gratitude regardless of outcome.

Signs of fear-based motivation include:
- Demanding complete clarity before moving
- Decisions driven primarily by avoiding pain
- Comparison and competition with others
- Goals that would prove worth rather than express it
- Inability to hold outcomes loosely

Signs of faith-based motivation include:
- Willingness to take steps with incomplete information
- Consulting God while moving forward
- Freedom from outcome attachment
- Goals aligned with serving God and others
- Peace that remains even if results disappoint

The practical application: before clarifying *what* someone feels called to do, excavate *why* they want it. The same external goal (launching a ministry, building a business, writing a book) can spring from radically different roots.

## The VIM model: vision requires intention and means

Dallas Willard's spiritual formation work offers a framework that bridges inspiration and implementation. His **VIM model**—Vision, Intention, Means—diagnoses why so many believers stall between sensing calling and living it.

**Vision** is a clear understanding of Kingdom reality and its relevance to life. Without compelling vision of who we could become in Christ and what God might accomplish through us, effort lacks direction. But vision alone is insufficient.

**Intention** is the resolute decision to pursue transformation. Willard diagnoses the core problem: "The problem of spiritual transformation among those who identify themselves as Christians today is not that it is impossible or that effectual means to it are not available. **The problem is that it is not intended.**" Many believers want transformation but haven't decided to pursue it—haven't crossed from desire to decision.

**Means** are the spiritual disciplines and practical actions that make transformation possible. "In the spiritual life it is actually true that 'where there is a will there is a way' because God is involved." Practices like solitude, fasting, study, prayer, and service create conditions for character change.

For workshop application, VIM suggests moving participants through stages: First, cultivate compelling vision of the God-aligned life. Second, push past vague wanting toward concrete decision—will you actually pursue this? Third, identify specific practices that create conditions for the calling to emerge.

## Vision precedes provision: the biblical pattern

A striking pattern runs through Scripture: **God reveals what He intends to do before He provides resources to accomplish it**. This is not incidental but formative—the gap between vision and fulfillment serves purposes that immediate provision would undermine.

Abraham received the promise of land and descendants at age 75; Isaac was born when he was **100—twenty-five years** of waiting. Joseph dreamed of leadership at 17; his brothers bowed before him **twenty-two years** later—after slavery, false accusation, and imprisonment. David was anointed as a youth; he was crowned king **fifteen or more years** later, spending the interim as a fugitive. Moses glimpsed his calling at 40; the burning bush came at 80—**forty years** of wilderness preparation.

Psalm 105:19 captures the purpose: "Until the time came to fulfill his dreams, the LORD tested Joseph's character." The gap is the workshop of character development. Abraham learned to trust despite impossibility. Joseph developed administrative skill and resilience in prison. David learned dependence on God while hiding in caves. Moses was stripped of Egyptian confidence to receive divine commission.

The Hebrew word *chazon* (vision) shares its root with the word for "provide"—to see is to have provision made. The English word "provision" itself means "for the vision"—supply that supports what has been seen. As one teacher summarizes: **"Your provision is in the vision."**

Nehemiah demonstrates the practical process: he received burden and vision while serving as cupbearer in Persia, prayed for four months before approaching the king, asked for both permission and provision (letters of safe passage, timber requisitions), surveyed the damage personally through night reconnaissance, then rallied others with the vision before walls were completed in fifty-two days. The sequence matters: revelation precedes resources.

This principle differs fundamentally from "name it and claim it" prosperity teaching. Biblical vision-precedes-provision originates with God rather than human desire, includes character formation rather than focusing on acquisition, involves waiting and testing rather than demanding instant manifestation, recognizes God's sovereignty over timing rather than treating human declaration as controlling, and produces humility rather than presumption.

## Navigating theological tensions across traditions

Christian traditions diverge significantly on vision, ambition, and calling. A workshop serving diverse believers must acknowledge rather than flatten these differences.

### The Reformed perspective emphasizes providence over personal vision

Luther revolutionized vocation theology by extending calling beyond clergy to all legitimate work: "God himself is milking the cows through the vocation of the milkmaid." Calvin extended this toward world transformation, seeing calling as divinely assigned station. Reformed thinkers are suspicious of subjective guidance and mystical impressions, preferring sanctified wisdom applied to revealed Scripture. The emphasis falls on faithfulness within assigned station rather than bold vision-casting.

Key voices like J.I. Packer warn: "The idea that the superior path in matters of guidance is to wait passively before God for direct promptings to action is a mistake." Reformed discernment asks whether a direction arises from faithful response to Scripture and whether one would be content serving in obscurity if God called there.

### The charismatic perspective expects supernatural guidance

Based on Acts 2's promise that "your young men shall see visions, and your old men shall dream dreams," charismatic teaching expects believers to hear God directly through prophecy, dreams, visions, and spiritual impression. Bold vision-casting is faithful response to God's promises. Wayne Grudem offers a moderate framework: prophecy is "spontaneous revelation from the Holy Spirit" but not equal to Scripture, and must be tested by the community.

This tradition asks different discernment questions: Does this align with prophetic words spoken over my life? Does my spirit bear witness with this direction? Am I willing to step out even without all the answers?

### The Catholic perspective prioritizes discernment of spirits

Ignatian spirituality provides the most developed discernment methodology, treating interior movements as data to be examined over time with spiritual directors. Decisions are never purely individual—Church authority and wise guides help test subjective impressions. Consolation and desolation function as diagnostic tools, but with sophistication: pseudo-consolation can begin well and lead astray.

The Catholic approach asks: Does this draw me closer to God or away? Is there lasting peace or restlessness? Have I tested this with a spiritual director? Am I interiorly free or attached to outcomes?

### The evangelical perspective centers on Scripture and wise counsel

Evangelicals typically emphasize Scripture-based discernment, personal relationship with God, and practical wisdom. The "open doors" approach looks for providential circumstances as guidance—opportunity plus opposition may indicate God's direction. John Piper's formulation: "Do the will you know. Discern the will you don't." Right discernment depends on "a mind so shaped and governed by the revealed will of God in the Bible that we see and assess all relevant factors with the mind of Christ."

### Key tensions to acknowledge

**Is vision received or discovered?** Charismatics expect direct revelation; Reformed traditions emphasize wisdom applied to Scripture; Catholics discern over time through interior movements; Evangelicals discover through Scripture, counsel, and circumstances.

**Can desires be trusted?** Reformed theology holds deep suspicion ("the heart is deceitful"); Charismatics may see desires as Spirit-led promptings; Catholics treat desires as data for discernment—neither trusted automatically nor dismissed; Evangelicals check desires against Scripture.

**Success as blessing or distraction?** Reformed "theology of the cross" sees suffering as normal and prosperity as spiritually dangerous; some charismatic teaching sees success as faith's fruit; Catholic saints' lives show suffering as sanctifying; Evangelicals generally reject prosperity gospel but may carry "soft" versions.

The wisest workshop approach allows participants to apply their tradition's wisdom while providing common ground: Scripture's teaching on selfish versus godly ambition, the value of community discernment, the necessity of testing impressions, and the dangers of both presumption and passivity.

## Practical framework for vision excavation workshops

Synthesizing these streams, a comprehensive vision excavation process might move through six phases:

### Phase 1: Preparing the heart (creating indifference)

Before excavating vision, participants need interior freedom from predetermined outcomes. Practices include extended silence, confession of attachments, and prayers of release. Key questions: What outcome am I clinging to? What am I afraid to hear God say? What would I be unwilling to do if God asked?

### Phase 2: Examining surface goals

Have participants list current goals and aspirations. For each, explore: Where did this goal originate? What feeling do I anticipate if I achieve it? What do I believe achieving this will prove or provide? Is there fear driving this goal?

### Phase 3: Excavating root desires

Use the "Five Whys" exercise—taking one surface goal and asking "why" progressively five times. Often the root reveals hunger for love, significance, security, freedom, or expression. Apply Augustine's diagnostic: Am I seeking from this goal what only God can give? What is the "god" behind this goal?

### Phase 4: Testing through consolation and desolation

Present several potential directions. For each, spend time in silence imagining this path, noticing whether you feel drawn toward God or away, more alive and connected or anxious and grasping. Warning signs (desolation): anxiety about proving yourself, comparison, scarcity thinking, obsessive control. Green lights (consolation): deep peace even if challenging, freedom from needing specific outcome, sense of alignment.

### Phase 5: Community discernment

Adapt the Quaker Clearness Committee model: a focus person brings a specific question; trusted others may only ask open, honest questions—no advice or judgments. The assumption: the person has an "Inner Teacher" who can guide them; the committee helps draw out that wisdom through deep listening and patient silence.

### Phase 6: Integration and writing the vision

Following Habakkuk 2:2, have participants write their vision clearly enough that "he who reads may run." This is not mission statement wordsmithing but clarifying what God has revealed sufficiently to recognize fulfillment. Include what God is doing *in* them, not just *for* them—character formation is as much the calling as external outcomes.

## Conclusion: excavation produces alignment, not just clarity

The goal of vision excavation is not merely discovering what to do but becoming the person who can steward divine calling. The process strips away false motivations, reveals root desires, tests impressions against Scripture and community wisdom, and cultivates interior freedom to receive whatever God reveals.

The biblical pattern suggests that vision and provision are linguistically and spiritually connected—*chazon* and *chazah* share the same root. When God gives vision, provision follows, though often through a character-forming gap. The waiting is not delay but preparation.

Andy Stanley captures the essence: "Vision is a clear mental picture of what could be, fueled by the conviction that it should be." The excavation process clarifies what *could* be by uncovering what already *is*—the calling God has planted beneath surface wants, fears, and cultural conditioning. What remains after excavation is not human ambition dressed in spiritual language but genuine response to divine invitation.

As Blackaby summarizes the paradigm shift: "The world operates on vision. God's people live by revelation." The difference determines everything.
